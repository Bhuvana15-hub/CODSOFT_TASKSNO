1. What is phishing?

Phishing is a trick where an attacker pretends to be someone you trust, such as a bank, a company or a colleague, to make you click a link, open a file or share private information like a password.

Why it works

Phishing targets people, not computers. Attackers create pressure and curiosity so that you act before you think.

Common types

  • Email phishing: fake emails sent to many people.
  • Spear phishing: a targeted email written for one person or company.
  • Smishing: phishing by SMS text message.
  • Vishing: phishing by phone call.

2. Spotting suspicious emails

Most phishing emails have at least one warning sign. Check these before you click anything.

Warning signs

  • Strange sender address: the display name looks right, but the email address behind it belongs to a different domain.
  • Urgent or scary language: "Your account will be closed in 24 hours!"
  • Generic greeting: "Dear customer" instead of your name.
  • Suspicious links: the text says one thing, but the real address (shown when you hover) is different.
  • Unexpected attachments: invoices, "documents" or "voice messages" you were not waiting for.
  • Requests for private data: real companies do not ask for your password, PIN or OTP by email.
  • Spelling and formatting mistakes: not always present, but common.

Example: a fake email (fictional company)

This example uses a made-up bank called ExampleBank. Read it, then look at the numbered red flags below it.

From: ExampleBank Support <support@examplebank-alerts.net> (1)

Subject: URGENT: Your account will be suspended (2)

Dear customer, (3)

We noticed unusual activity on your account. You must verify your details within 24 hours or your account will be closed. (2)

Click here to verify now: http://examplebank-verify-login.net/secure (4)

Please also confirm your password and the OTP we will send you. (5)

Attachment: Account_Statement.zip (6)

Red flags in the example

  1. The sender domain is examplebank-alerts.net, not the bank's real domain.
  2. Urgency and a threat push you to act without thinking.
  3. A generic greeting. A real bank knows your name.
  4. The link goes to a look-alike domain, not the bank's real website.
  5. Asking for a password and OTP. A bank never asks for these.
  6. A .zip attachment you did not expect can contain malware.

What to do

Do not click or open anything. Open your bank's official app or type its website address yourself, and check your account there. Then report and delete the email.

3. Fake login pages and fraudulent websites

Attackers copy the look of a real login page so that you type your password into their website instead. The page can look perfect, so the address bar is the thing to check.

How to check a website

  • Read the domain carefully. The real site name is the part just before the last dot-and-ending, for example examplebank.com.
  • Read it from right to left. In examplebank.com.secure-verify.net, the real domain is secure-verify.net, not examplebank.com.
  • Watch for look-alike spelling (called typosquatting), such as examp1ebank.com with the number 1 instead of the letter l.
  • Do not trust the padlock alone. The padlock (HTTPS) only means the connection is encrypted. Attackers can get it for fake sites too.
  • Do not log in from a link. Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark or the official app.
  • Be careful with shortened links that hide the real destination.

Signs of a fraudulent shopping or service website

  • Prices that are far too good to be true
  • No contact details, or only a free email address
  • Only unusual payment methods are accepted (such as gift cards or direct transfers)
  • The site was created very recently and has no reviews you can verify

4. Social engineering techniques

Social engineering means manipulating people, not machines. Attackers use normal human feelings to make you skip your usual caution.

TechniqueHow it worksExample
Urgency Pressure to act right now "Pay in 1 hour or your service will be cut."
Fear A threat of loss or punishment "Your account has been hacked. Click to secure it."
Authority Pretending to be a boss, the police or the IT team "This is the CEO. Buy gift cards for a client today."
Greed Promising a prize or easy money "You won a lottery. Pay a small fee to claim it."
Curiosity A tempting attachment or link "Photos of you from last night."
Helpfulness and trust Asking for a small favour or "help" "I am locked out, can you share your code?"

Other tricks

  • Pretexting: inventing a believable story, such as "I am from IT support and need to fix your VPN."
  • Impersonation: using a colleague's or company's name and style.
  • Baiting: leaving something tempting, like an unknown USB drive.

The rule: when a message makes you feel rushed, scared or very excited, stop. That feeling is the warning sign.

5. Practical security tips

Everyday habits

  • Pause before you click. Check the sender, the link and what is being asked.
  • Go to the source yourself. Type the website address or use the official app instead of following a link.
  • Verify through another channel. If your "boss" or "bank" asks for something unusual, call them on a number you already know.
  • Never share passwords, PINs or OTPs with anyone, even if they say they are from support.
  • Hover over links to see the real address before clicking.
  • Be careful with attachments you did not expect.

Protect your accounts

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) so a stolen password alone is not enough.
  • Use a password manager. It creates strong, unique passwords, and it normally fills them in only on the correct website, which can help you notice a fake one.
  • Use a different password for each account.
  • Keep your phone, browser and apps updated.

If you clicked or entered details

  1. Disconnect from the internet if you opened a suspicious file.
  2. Change the password for that account right away, and for any other account that uses the same password.
  3. Turn on MFA and check the account for activity you do not recognize.
  4. If money or banking details are involved, call your bank immediately.
  5. Report it. At work, tell your IT or security team. In India, you can report cyber fraud on the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (cybercrime.gov.in) or call the helpline 1930.
  6. Do not feel embarrassed. Fast reporting limits the damage.

6. Case studies

These are real incidents. They show that phishing affects even technology companies with strong security teams.

Case 1: Twitter, July 2020 (phone phishing)

What happened: On 14 and 15 July 2020, attackers phoned Twitter employees while pretending to be the company's IT help desk. They said there was a problem with the VPN and sent the employees to a fake login page that looked like the real one. The employees entered their credentials, and the attackers used them to reach internal tools.

Result: Many high-profile accounts were taken over and used to post a bitcoin scam. New York's financial regulator reported that the scammers took about $118,000 in bitcoin.

Lessons:

  • Phishing can happen by phone, not just email (vishing).
  • A caller claiming to be "IT" must still be verified through an official channel.
  • Login pages on look-alike domains can steal credentials from trained staff too.

Sources: AP via TechXplore, Help Net Security

Case 2: Google and Facebook invoice scam, 2013 to 2015

What happened: A man in Lithuania, Evaldas Rimasauskas, pretended to be Quanta Computer, a real hardware supplier that Google and Facebook regularly paid. He sent fake emails with forged invoices, contracts and letters asking for payment to bank accounts he controlled.

Result: Employees at both companies paid the fake invoices, and the scheme brought in more than $100 million in total. He was arrested in 2017, extradited to the United States and pleaded guilty in 2019. Google said it recovered its money, and Facebook said it recovered most of its money.

Lessons:

  • Phishing is also about money. This type is often called business email compromise.
  • Always confirm changes to payment details by calling a known number.
  • Invoices should go through a proper approval process, not just one person's inbox.

Sources: NPR, CNBC
